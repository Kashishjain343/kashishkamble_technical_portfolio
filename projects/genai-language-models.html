Building and fine-tuning a small language model

A hands-on look at how language models actually learn: from simple word-pair models to a small BERT-style model trained from scratch and put to work on real drug reviews.

The business question: Can a small model you train yourself handle a real text task, or is it always better to reuse a large pretrained model? This project tests that by building both and comparing them on the same job: classifying patient drug reviews.

Step 1: The basics

Built n-gram (word-pair) language models and compared them using perplexity, a measure of how surprised a model is by new text. Lower is better. This sets a simple baseline for what "understanding language" means to a model.

Step 2: Pretraining

Pretrained a small BERT-style model ("nano-BERT") on WikiText-2, a public collection of Wikipedia articles. The model learns language by guessing hidden words in sentences, the same idea behind full-size BERT.

Step 3: Fine-tuning

Fine-tuned the pretrained model to classify DrugLib patient reviews of medications. This is where a general language model gets turned into a tool that does one specific, useful job.

Step 4: Benchmarking

Compared the fine-tuned nano-BERT against simpler classifiers and against bert-base-uncased, a much larger model pretrained by Google, to see how much size and pretraining data really matter.

Why it matters for business

Companies sit on huge amounts of text: customer reviews, support tickets, survey comments. This project covers the full path from raw text to a working classifier, and the trade-off every team faces: build a small model they fully control, or adapt a large pretrained one that costs more to run.
